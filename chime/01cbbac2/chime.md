> *There is something fishy about probability. I mean, ok, if you roll a dice over and over it makes sense. If you write down the outcome you will see a pattern emerge where each face of the dice will, over time, turn out an equal amount of times. But really, what does that tell you for a single roll of the dice?*

> *My suspicion here is that the probability of an outcome applies only for similar events that occur over and over. The probability of an outcome emerges only as a property of the long series of outcomes. And a strategy where you choose how to act based on the mathematical probability of each outcome will be yours only if you do it over and over. But from this it is not clear to me if the probability says anything about a single outcome?*

> *The popular example where this comes up is in the famous "Monty Hall Problem" (See <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monty_Hall_problem> ).*

> *"Suppose you're on a game show, and you're given the choice of three doors: Behind one door is a car; behind the others, goats. You pick a door, say No. 1, and the host, who knows what's behind the doors, opens another door, say No. 3, which has a goat. He then says to you, "Do you want to pick door No. 2?" Is it to your advantage to switch your choice?"*

> *And the popular answer is that after having been shown what door does NOT hide the price you should switch to the third yet unchosen door. We calculate that the probability the price if behind the unchosen door a f t e r eliminating one door goes up to two thirds while the door you picked keeps having a one third probability it hides the price. *

> *But here is the crux! We can safely say that if you should play this game over and over you will find the the number of times you choose the price will get doubled if you always switch compared if you never switch. That is, I claim, what the probability tells you. The probability is the emergent property of the many outcomes.*

> *Now, what does this probability tell you if you play alone this game only once? I mean, if you play only once the probability that tells you something about the distribution over many plays does not yet apply! You have only this game!*

> *I think I am saying that there IS something fishy to state that the probability of s ingle outcome actually applies to that outcome alone. Is it not more true to say that we model the distribution of outcomes by assigning a probability to each outcome although this value says nothing if you do NOT repeat the action?*

> *Now, let's say the games get played by many players but you play only once yourself. Then can we say that you should switch so that you find yourself in the group of players where half are winners compared to staying in the group where one third are winners? I find even this unconvincing when it comes to your single choice in your single play. Remember, the probability only applies to the distribution of winners, not to you personally. It seems to me to still be the case you must play yourself many times to reap the benefit of always switching**. Only if you play many times yourself will the probability emerge and affect your outcome?*

> *To make you benefit from switching doors to become a member of the "two third winners" group applies only IF this group shares the winning with you! That is, there are in fact many plays off the game. So the probability DOES in fact emerge to provide the switching group with a higher winning rate. And because this group shares the winnings in the group you want to be in it. The hidden prerequisite here is that "your group" decides collectively to act in this way b e f o r e playing the game!*

> *Maybe the illusion here is that normally we engage in activities that does repeat? And normally we are members of groups that does share (society, family, tribe...). So we may just as well act "as if" every choice you make should be guided by the probability that tells something about the distribution of outcomes over time?*

> *But still, I claim for events that truly happen only once to you, the probability of each outcome does in fact not apply. Your knowledge on how the outcomes distribute over a large number of tries sais nothing for your single actualised outcome! *

> *The example of the latter is the reasoning about how probable it is that we live in a simulation. Or that we are alone in the universe. Or that we live near the beginning or the end of the life of the universe. Here I argue that the probability we imagine to assign to find ourselves in any specific reality in fact says nothing about what reality we actually are in. Only if we live over and over (plays the game many times) or share the outcome with a group applying the same strategy ( all currently living human beings) should the probability tell us anything about what reality we are in?*
