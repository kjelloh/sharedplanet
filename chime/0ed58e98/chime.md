I find it an interesting and possibly valuable idea to address the meaning crisis with a need to reconnect with death.

1) Consider things have to die for life to prosper and become more meaningful?

> *So the idea here is to propose that we need to make friends with death again. Not only to foster a good relationship with the fact that we ourselves will die one day. But also as a general idea that stuff we surround us with also have to die for things we value to gain more value. And no, I am not thinking of planned obsolescence. I am after the intuition that there is some truth to discover in the fact that living individuals die not because their DNA carries a code for planned obsolescence but because living things are provided a restricted life span to spend on transforming reality from what it is into what it should be. And the very reason the provided life span is restricted hides some crucial fact about the value of death?*

> *But I want to start off with something more easy. I find it disturbing and troublesome that my digital stuff does not die in any for me valuable way. Instead digital stuff I care about usually dies because I loose, I make some error or the environment in which it lives gets to hostile for it to survive. So what do I mean with that. Well, take my digital photos as an example. They are stored good knows where in a format who knows I may be able to read when times come I would like too. It is the very reason they exist in a reality that is not as stable as our "real" reality that causes them to die. If I store them on a hard drive I can not be sure that hard drive will be readable by any computer in the future. And even if the drive is readable I do not know if there will be any future computer program that can understand the digital file content to recreate the photo to me?*

> *On the other hand, stuff I do NOT care about anymore can carry on living although I really would like this stuff to die off. Having old emails piling up to gigabytes over the years is just not convenient. Not to mention old files that just pile up in my personal folders and carried over from computer to computer as I move on in life and to new hardwares. It seems to me I would welcome some mechanism that would cause digital data to die with old age in some for me predictable way?*

> *But how should stuff die in a way so that valuable stuff survives and stuff that does not matter so much just dies off? Can we reverse engineer the reason living things die and apply this "time to die" mechanism also to stuff humans produce?*

2) Consider we need a way to apply reason into a context where "the way stuff should die" can emerge (not be planned)?

> *The idea here is to make a case for prioritising that which emerge before that which we plan? It seems to me that the power of life to home in on the faith that does recurse is tightly bound to observing and evaluating the emergent collective behaviour and result? As if that which emerges is the feed back of what reality is actually made off? As if that which emerges is the result of running an algorithm in reality itself while that which we plan is the result of running an algorithm on an abstraction of reality. It is only by observing that which emerges that we know how well our abstraction of reality actually is?*

> *If that which emerge is more "real" than our model of reality that enables us to plan (simulate reality) then it seems to follow that we need to reason in a model where we take emergence into account and also values it more than our model itself. Now this seems tricky? We need to convince ourselves that the model we are using to reason about reality must contain an axiom that the model must be updated to match that which emerges. As if we need to trust a model that itself tells us to NOT trust the model. And the reason we must do this is because such a model is more likely to produce recursive acts-in-faith than a rigid model does?*

> *I can't help but have a strong intuition that the reason we need to trust death to be a crucial part of the "algorithm" of life is that by having individual die life ensures that that which survives is more in tune with that which recurse in presence of that which emerge. It is a crucial part in homing in on a model of reality that takes into account not only the value of each individual life in its life span but also the value of the collective lives and the collectives life span. But it does not stop there. By having individuals die life ensures that which survives is some model of reality that take into account reality itself and ensures that this model lives in some form in each individual but also in the collective. In a way life is a collective stores a recurse-able model of reality encoded into reality itself by running the model on reality itself.*

3) Consider to compare "stuff needs to die" with "planned obsolescence" (<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planned_obsolescence> )?

==&gt; Consider it is important what mechanism it is that causes stuff to die (they should die to make you value them not because someone else needs to sell you a new one)?

> *So what exactly is the right reason for stuff to die if planned obsolescence is the wrong one? For one we can start off with the intuition and feeling that a thing that dies because it was planned to do so just feels wrong. So what is this feeling telling us about the "wrong-ness" of things dying because they where manufactured to die? I mean, in a way we can say that all living things seems to also come into existence with a built in life span. so they too are "manufactured" with a "planned obsolescence"? Maybe it is that we do not want stuff to die because they become obsolete, we want them to die when they fulfilled their purpose? But that seems a bit lame and unsatisfactory as an explanation. Maybe we have an intuition that living things are "there ought to be" actors and as such they "need" a restricted life span in which to give it a go to transform reality into what it ought to be. And when they have had a sufficient life span to do so they should die to let new actors take over. When looked at in this way the life span is like the amount of time you have been given to spend on transforming reality. And the reason you are given a limited amount of time to spend is that if you transform reality in a "bad" way your death will ensure your way of acting in the world is killed off. The trade off is that if your way of transforming reality is a "good one" your death will terminate also this "good" transformation. But then, the very mechanism to evaluate if your contribution to the order of things is that those left alive will mimic your actions. And this in itself ensures that the reason your action was "good" was not because you were special in carrying these actions out but rather that your way could be mimicked and carried on by others?*

> *So the reason planned obsolescence is "bad" is that it is not sensitive to the value of it existing. Planned obsolescence is driven by incitement to provide the shortest possible life span for stuff that you as the producer can get away with. And this is the wrong value to foster for determining what stuff should exist? Living things on the other hand are sensitive to the result or value of them being alive as valued by other living things. And the very evaluation process includes death of the individual in favour of survival of the act-in-faith that individuals leave behind. Life homes in on the faith that recurse over time while planned obsolescence just maximises the consumption of resources at the owners expense?*
