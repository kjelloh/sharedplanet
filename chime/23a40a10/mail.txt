I found it perplexing when a coffee expert claimed that you need more coffee in an infusion process than in a percolation one (when it feels like the infusion should do a better job of extracting more from the coffee into the water)?

1) Consider this youtube video “Coffee Brewing Ratios Explained” where a coffee expert claims you need more coffee, not less, when brewing using infusion as opposed to percolation (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ipB6P1uzNYM <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ipB6P1uzNYM> )?

	==> Consider this explanation about why an infusion process requires more coffee than percolation to produce coffee of the same strength (https://youtu.be/ipB6P1uzNYM?t=395 <https://youtu.be/ipB6P1uzNYM?t=395> )?

“Now, you can broadly divide all coffee brewing methods into two camps, percolation and infusion. Now with percolation water is passing through a bed of coffee. In infusion all of the water and all of the coffee are hanging out together during the brew time. A pour over is percolation. A French press and arrow press those are infusion methods. Now, I would recommend using a  little bit more coffee for an infusion method than I would for a pour over, for a percolation method. I explain why. Let’s imagine for the sake of argument we’re going to brew a liter of coffee and we're going to use 60 grams per liter. It's a great starting point. And in this theoretical brew we're going to extract 20% of the coffee, right? So it means that of those 60 grams about 12 grams were dissolved and brought into the liquid coffee. Now, when you brew a percolation, you brew a pour over, not all of the water that you pour in ends up in the brew. Typically about 2 grams per gram of coffee get absorbed by the ground coffee, that bed still contains a good amount of water. And that water never really got involved in the brewing process. So what you have in the resulting brew is 12 grams of coffee dissolved in roundabout 880 grams of water. If you take a French press and you do the same thing. You brew 60 grams per liter those 12 grams of soluble that would be extracted, that 20% extraction, that's now dissolved into a thousand grams of water. All thousand grams were involved in the brewing process. And those solubles are distributed amongst the whole thing. That makes that brew weaker even though it’s the same extraction. That’s why if you want a similar extraction and a similar strength I would recommend using more coffee; In my infusions 70 grams maybe 75 grams per liter of water."

		 
		Claim 1: In percolation Typically about 2 grams per gram of coffee get absorbed by the ground coffee (and will NOT end up in the brew)

		
		Claim 2: In infusion the extracted coffee is distributed into the whole volume of water

	==> It seems his claim is that in both cases the same amount of coffee is extracted but in infusion it is distributed in a larger volume of water?

		Q: Why does he seem to claim that in infusion the water trapped in the coffee does not matter (while in peculation its kept separate from the brew)?
		...

	==> Aha - He is correct! In both cases we get 880 grams of brewed coffee (120 g ware trapped in the coffee) but percolation extracts more coffee into the brew!

		==> In percolation the 120 g of trapped water will have lower coffee concentration than the same amount of water trapped in infused coffee?
		==> Thus the final 880 grams of brew will carry more extracted coffee in the percolation process than in the infusion process?

2) Consider why it is NOT the case that infusion potentially being able to extract more of the coffee into the water thus will require less coffee to brew the same strength?

	==> Intuitively it feels like infusion would extract more coffee because it feels like in percolation some extraction will remain together with the stare trapped in the coffee?

	==> So it seems our intuition is wrong in that the extraction differs (we actually extracts the same amount of coffee in infusion as in percolation)?

	==> But then why is there less water trapped in the coffee after an infusion process than in a percolation one?

	==> AHA - In infusion the water trapped in the coffee has a higher concentration of coffee than the corresponding water in the peculation process?

It seems the key observation here is that in the peculation process the water that extracts the coffee if always fresh water. So it will be able to delete the coffee better and bring all of the extracted coffee down into the brew.

But in the infusion process an equilibrium will settle between all the water and coffee. The coffee extracted from the coffee will stop when the water trapped in the coffee has the same coffee concentration as the water surrounding it?

Thus the water trapped in the coffee is of the same amount but of different coffee concentration in the two processes. Peculation is able to extract more coffee into the brew because it is always fresh water involved in the extraction!

3) It seems the logical fallacy our intuition is telling us is that percolation for some reason is less efficient when in fact it is the opposite?

	==> It seems out intuition tells us it is more efficient to have the coffee really soak in the water (that this will extract more coffee)?

	==> But our intuition seems to not take into account that when soaking the equilibrium will prohibit all of the coffee to be extracted in the soaking process?

	==> It seems our intuition fails to realise that in percolation the extraction is always made with fresh ware which maximise the extraction process?

	==> It seems our intuition tells us percolation is less efficient because it feels like not all coffee is involved (or the time for coffee to be extracted is less in percolation)?

	==> Consider this is an example of where our emotions “reasons” with more-time-is-better while our rational reasoning can take into account a more abstract notion of extraction efficiency (more-in-less-time-possible algorithm exists)?
