- The **Modified Julian Date** (MJD) was introduced by the Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory in 1957 to record the orbit of [Sputnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sputnik_1) via an [IBM 704](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_704) (36-bit machine) and using only 18 bits until August 7, 2576. MJD is the epoch of VAX/VMS and its successor [OpenVMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenVMS#Timekeeping), using 63-bit date/time, which allows times to be stored up to July 31, 31086, 02:48:05.47.[&#91;11&#93;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_day#cite_note-11) The MJD has a starting point of midnight on November 17, 1858 and is computed by MJD = JD - 2400000.5 [&#91;12&#93;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_day#cite_note-12)
- The **Truncated Julian Day** (TJD) was introduced by [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA)/[Goddard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goddard_Space_Flight_Center) in 1979 as part of a parallel grouped binary time code (PB-5) "designed specifically, although not exclusively, for spacecraft applications." TJD was a 4-digit day count from MJD 40000, which was May 24, 1968, represented as a 14-bit binary number. Since this code was limited to four digits, TJD recycled to zero on MJD 50000, or October 10, 1995, "which gives a long ambiguity period of 27.4 years". (NASA codes PB-1—PB-4 used a 3-digit day-of-year count.) Only whole days are represented. Time of day is expressed by a count of seconds of a day, plus optional milliseconds, microseconds and nanoseconds in separate fields. Later PB-5J was introduced which increased the TJD field to 16 bits, allowing values up to 65535, which will occur in the year 2147. There are five digits recorded after TJD 9999.[&#91;13&#93;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_day#cite_note-13)[&#91;14&#93;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_day#cite_note-14)
- The **Dublin Julian Date** (DJD) is the number of days that has elapsed since the epoch of the solar and lunar [ephemerides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephemerides) used from 1900 through 1983, [Newcomb's Tables of the Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcomb%27s_Tables_of_the_Sun) and [Ernest W. Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_W._Brown)'s *Tables of the Motion of the Moon* (1919). This epoch was noon UT on [January 0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_0), 1900, which is the same as noon UT on December 31, 1899. The DJD was defined by the International Astronomical Union at their meeting in [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin), [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland), in 1955.[&#91;15&#93;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_day#cite_note-15)

- The [Lilian day number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lilian_date) is a count of days of the Gregorian calendar and not defined relative to the Julian Date. It is an integer applied to a whole day; day 1 was October 15, 1582, which was the day the Gregorian calendar went into effect. The original paper defining it makes no mention of the time zone, and no mention of time-of-day.[&#91;16&#93;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_day#cite_note-16) It was named for [Aloysius Lilius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aloysius_Lilius), the principal author of the Gregorian calendar.[&#91;17&#93;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_day#cite_note-17)
- [Rata Die](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rata_Die) is a system used in [Rexx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rexx), [Go](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_(programming_language)>) and [Microsoft .NET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_.NET). It uses the local timezone, and day 1 is January 1, 1, that is, the first day of the [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Era) or [Common Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era) in the [proleptic Gregorian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proleptic_Gregorian_calendar).[&#91;18&#93;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_day#cite_note-18)
