> *So, the basic idea here is to ponder on the possibility to instruct a human to perform a build task using some set of standardised movement of body and limbs in conjunction with a defined workplace and tool set?*

> *Like, to cut panels from a stock sheet may involve a work table, a track saw and a set of movements by a human to place the sheet, measure, place the track saw and make the cuts in some "optimal" way?*

> *I imagine an existing CAM tool could be extended with a "build" generator aiming at instructing a human (instead of say a CNC) to perform the cuts in an accurate and easy way?*

1) Consider to name the standardised instructions to a human the "H-code" as a reference to the "G-Code" sent to a CNC or 3D printer?

> *As for G-code that "...**tells the motors where to move, how fast to move, and what path to follow" we can imagine the H-code that "**tells the human where to move body and limbs, how fast to move, and what path to follow**" as well as what tools to use and how to confugure them?*

> *It seems to me that for G-code you already "know" the machine properties and traits as well as the tool configuration (e.g., what router bit is inserted into a CNC or what filament material inserted into a 3D printer). So the G-code is generated with these assumptions taken into account?*

> *For a human "H-code" we need a way to define the same parameters to define the tools used by the human as well as the workplace environment (e.g., bench dimensions and location, tool properties and tool environments used)?*

> *But basically the H-code to instruct a human could be inspired by the G-code (if there are any already defined) for a human torso robot?*

> *On the other side, a human probably needs "detailed enough" instructions on how to move and NOT "dumb" move-this-joint-this-angle-with-this-velocity directives.*

2) Consider the wikipedia page on G-code (<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-code> )?

> *"**G-code instructions are provided to a machine controller (industrial computer) that **tells the motors where to move, how fast to move, and what path to follow**. The two most common situations are that, within a machine tool such as a lathe or mill, a cutting tool is moved according to these instructions through a toolpath cutting away material to leave only the finished workpiece and/or an unfinished workpiece is precisely positioned in any of up to nine axes&#91;1&#93; around the three dimensions relative to a toolpath and, either or both can move relative to each other. The same concept also extends to noncutting tools such as forming or burnishing tools, photoplotting, additive methods such as 3D printing, and measuring instruments.**"*
